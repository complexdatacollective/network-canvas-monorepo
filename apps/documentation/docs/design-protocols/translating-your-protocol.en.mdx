<Definition>
  A protocol can be written in more than one language. Participants take the
  interview in one of your protocol's languages, and see your prompts, labels,
  options and other text in that language.
</Definition>

You write your protocol in one language first, and then translate its text into each of the other languages you want to offer. Architect keeps every translation of a text together, and shows you which texts still need translating.

<TipBox>

Translations need [schema 9](/en/get-started/protocol-schema-information#schema-version-9). Build your protocol in Architect, and run it in Fresco 5.0 or later, or Interviewer 9.0 or later. Architect Classic and Interviewer Classic can't open a schema 9 protocol.

</TipBox>

## What can be translated

Every text that participants see can be translated. This includes:

- stage names, which participants see in the interview's list of stages
- prompts, introduction panels and panel titles
- form titles, and the questions and hints of form fields, including the labels and hints of [Network Composer](/en/design-protocols/interface-documentation/network-composer) fields
- the labels of response options, and the labels at each end of a slider
- the titles, text and resource descriptions of Information screens
- the text of other interfaces, such as the explanation on an [Anonymisation](/en/design-protocols/interface-documentation/anonymisation) screen
- the labels of node types and edge types (see [Labels for node types and edge types](#labels-for-node-types-and-edge-types))
- the labels of the attributes a [Narrative](/en/design-protocols/interface-documentation/narrative) preset highlights (see [Labels for highlighted attributes on a Narrative stage](#labels-for-highlighted-attributes-on-a-narrative-stage))

Some parts of a protocol are the same in every language:

- **Names and values.** Attribute names, node and edge type names, and option values are what your exported data uses, so they don't change between languages. Only the labels participants see are translated.
- **Attribute labels.** An attribute's label is a readable name for it, in plain text, and it isn't translated. Participants don't see it: every [Network Composer](/en/design-protocols/interface-documentation/network-composer) field needs a label of its own, which you can translate.
- **Resources.** Images, videos, audio, rosters and maps are the same in every language. If a resource contains words, choose one that works for all of your participants, or explain it in text that you can translate.
- **The interview's own buttons and messages**, such as navigation, help and validation messages. Network Canvas provides these itself, in a fixed set of languages (see [How participants get their language](#how-participants-get-their-language)).

## Choosing your protocol's languages

When you create a protocol in Architect, the **Create New Protocol** dialog asks for its **Protocol languages**: the languages participants can take it in. Architect suggests the language its own interface is shown in. To offer more languages, search for and choose them in the list.

If you choose more than one language, the dialog also asks for the **Default language** (see [The default language and which translation participants see](#the-default-language-and-which-translation-participants-see)). Architect suggests the language its interface is shown in if you chose it, or otherwise one of the languages you chose. If you choose only one language, it is the default language.

You can change the languages and the default language later, on the **Languages** tab.

A protocol you start from a template is already written in a language, so Architect doesn't ask. The templates that come with Architect are written in US English (`en-US`).

## Managing your protocol's languages

Open the **Languages** tab in the protocol's navigation, between **Codebook** and **Summary**. The **Protocol languages** section lists each language your protocol is written in. For each one you can see:

- its name, its name in its own language, and its language code, such as `es` or `pt-BR`
- a **Default** badge on the default language
- how many of the protocol's texts are translated into it, such as "12 of 40 texts translated"

Everything else you can do with a language is in its actions menu, the **⋯** button at the end of its row: **Make default**, **Relabel translations…** and **Remove**.

Above the list, **Which translation participants see** opens a short summary of [The default language and which translation participants see](#the-default-language-and-which-translation-participants-see).

Languages have no order. Architect lists them alphabetically by name, in the language Architect's interface is shown in, and the order your protocol stores them in makes no difference to what participants see.

You can also manage your protocol's languages from a [Language Chooser](/en/design-protocols/interface-documentation/language-chooser) stage's editor. It shows the same list, with the same rules, as the **Languages** tab, so you can manage your protocol's languages without leaving your stages. Everything below applies in both places. A change you make there also applies to the stage's changes that you haven't saved yet. Missing translations are listed only on the **Languages** tab.

### Adding languages

Click **Add languages**, then search for and choose one or more languages in the **Languages** list. Click **Add languages** to finish.

A new language starts with no translations. Until you translate a text, participants who use the new language see another language's translation of it instead (see [The default language and which translation participants see](#the-default-language-and-which-translation-participants-see)).

### The default language and which translation participants see

The default language is the one participants start the interview in when their browser lists none of your protocol's languages. To change it, choose **Make default** from another language's actions menu.

The default language doesn't have to hold every text. A text needs a translation in at least one of your protocol's languages, and it can be any of them. A missing translation, whether in the default language or in another, is only a warning (see [Finding missing translations](#finding-missing-translations)).

For each text, participants see the first of these that has a translation:

1. **Their own language.** This is the language the interview is shown in: the one they chose on a [Language Chooser](/en/design-protocols/interface-documentation/language-chooser) stage, or else the best match among the languages their browser lists, or else the default language (see [How participants get their language](#how-participants-get-their-language)). A language matches exactly, or by its closest related language. For example, European Portuguese (`pt-PT`) is closest to Brazilian Portuguese (`pt-BR`), Mexican Spanish (`es-MX`) is closest to Spanish (`es`), and `zh-TW` is closest to Traditional Chinese (`zh-Hant`).
2. **Each other language their browser lists**, in the order the browser lists them. Each one matches exactly or by its closest related language, in the same way.
3. **The default language**, matching exactly or by its closest related language.
4. **Any other language of your protocol** that has the text.

Which languages are closely related comes from the language matching data of the Unicode CLDR project. It also treats some languages as acceptable substitutes for each other, such as Swiss German (`gsw`) for German (`de`).

For example, say your protocol is written in English (the default), French and Brazilian Portuguese, and the question "How often do you see them?" is translated into English and Brazilian Portuguese, but not into French. Participants see the question like this:

| The participant                                                          | Sees                 | Why                                                                                                                                                                 |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Their browser is set to European Portuguese (`pt-PT`)                    | Brazilian Portuguese | European Portuguese isn't one of your languages, so the interview uses its closest related language, Brazilian Portuguese.                                          |
| Uses French, and their browser lists no other language your protocol has | English              | French has no translation, and neither does any other language in their browser that your protocol has, so they see the default language.                           |
| Uses French, and their browser also lists Portuguese                     | Brazilian Portuguese | French has no translation, so the interview tries the other languages their browser lists before the default language, and Portuguese matches Brazilian Portuguese. |

If the question were written only in Brazilian Portuguese, a participant using English would still see it, because no other language has it.

When your protocol has more than one variant of a language, such as Spanish (`es`) and Mexican Spanish (`es-MX`), a participant whose own language is one of them sees the text in that variant if it has it, or else in the other variant, before any other language.

### Relabelling a language's translations

**Relabel translations…**, in a language's actions menu, marks every text written in that language as written in another. Use it when the translations are really in another language or regional variant, for example if you added Spanish (`es`) but wrote your translations for participants in Mexico (`es-MX`). Nothing is translated or deleted: only the language the text is marked as changes. You can't relabel translations as a language the protocol already has.

### Removing a language

Choose **Remove** from a language's actions menu to stop offering it. Architect asks you to confirm, because every translation written in that language is deleted, and participants can no longer choose it.

You can't remove:

- the default language. Make another language the default first.
- a language that some texts are written only in. Translate those texts into another language first, so that participants always have something to read.

For these languages, **Remove** is unavailable, and the menu says why.

## Finding missing translations

Missing translations don't stop you from saving, previewing or using your protocol, because participants see another language's translation of the text instead (see [The default language and which translation participants see](#the-default-language-and-which-translation-participants-see)). They are still worth finishing before you collect data.

Architect shows you where they are:

- The **Languages** tab shows a warning icon while some translations are missing.
- The protocol overview screen shows a notice with the number of missing translations, and a **Go to Languages** link.
- The **Missing translations** section of the **Languages** tab lists the texts that have no translation in one language at a time. It is a tree: **Stages**, **Codebook** and **Protocol** branch into the places that hold the texts. A stage is named by its position and interface, such as "Stage 4 · Language Chooser", and a node type or edge type by its name. Each text is a row that previews what participants see in its place.

Choose the language whose gaps you want to see under **Show missing translations for**, or click the button next to the language in the **Protocol languages** list, such as **Show 3 missing French translations**.

Click a row to open its translation dialog. It has the text's field, with a language menu, so you can write the text in every language, and saving writes them all at once. Beside it, **What participants see** shows the text as participants using each of your protocol's languages will see it, as you type. It marks the default language and the language you are editing. Under a language that has no translation of the text, a note says what participants see instead:

- When the text is shown in a closely related language, such as Spanish for Mexican Spanish, the note reads "Not translated yet. Shown in Spanish."
- Otherwise, the note adds that this is what participants see unless their browser also lists a language that has the text, because the other languages in a participant's browser are tried before the default language.

## Translating text

You translate a text in the same place where you wrote it, such as the stage editor. When your protocol has more than one language, each text field has a language menu next to it, showing the language you are editing. Choose another language from the menu to write in that language.

All the text fields in an editor switch language together, so you can work through a stage in one language at a time. The editor always opens in the default language, and its previews show the text in the language you are editing.

The language menu tells you how each text is doing:

- each language in the menu is tagged **Default** or **Missing**, where that applies
- a badge next to the menu counts the languages this text is missing in, such as "2 translations missing"
- under a text that has no translation in the language you are editing, a note says which language's text participants using that language will see instead, such as "Not translated into French yet. Participants using French will see the English text". Unless the text is shown in a closely related language, the note continues ", unless their browser also lists a language that has it", because the other languages in a participant's browser are tried before the default language

To remove a translation, delete all of its text. A protocol with only one language doesn't show language menus at all.

### Labels for node types and edge types

Each node type and edge type has a label: the words participants are shown for it. The label is kept separate from the name, which your exported data uses, so you can translate the label without changing your data. A new node type or edge type starts with its name as its label.

To edit a type's label, open the **Codebook** page and click **Edit entity** on the type. The label is under the type's name.

When your protocol has more than one language, a label has a language menu like any other text. A label needs text in at least one language. Any language you leave empty counts as a missing translation, just like other text.

Attributes have labels too, which you edit on the **Codebook** page with the label button (a tag icon) next to the attribute. An attribute's label is plain text, so it has no language menu and is never a missing translation.

### Labels for highlighted attributes on a Narrative stage

A [Narrative](/en/design-protocols/interface-documentation/narrative) preset can highlight the nodes that have one or more true-or-false attributes. During the interview, the preset switcher lists these attributes by their labels, so that participants can choose which one to highlight.

You write these labels in the Narrative stage editor. Open the preset, and under **Node highlighting**, each attribute you tick has a **Label for "…"** field, named after the attribute. The label starts as the attribute's name, and it has a language menu like any other text. Every highlighted attribute needs a label.

## Identifying the language of an older protocol

Protocols made before Network Canvas recorded which language a protocol is written in have all their text marked as an unspecified language, with the language code `und`. This happens when Architect, Fresco or Interviewer upgrades a protocol to schema 9. Your text isn't changed: Network Canvas just doesn't know which language it's written in.

Architect lists this language as **Unspecified language**, and participants on a [Language Chooser](/en/design-protocols/interface-documentation/language-chooser) screen see the same name.

Tell Architect which language your text is really written in before you add translations:

1. Open the **Languages** tab. A notice asks "Which language is your text written in?".
2. Click **Identify language**.
3. Choose the language from the **Language** list.
4. Click **Identify language** to finish.

Every text marked as unspecified is now marked as the language you chose. Nothing is translated or deleted. Until you do this, the protocol overview screen shows the notice "Identify the language of your protocol", and interviews run with the protocol record `und` as their language in your exported data.

## Previewing a language

To check your translations as participants will see them, [preview](/en/design-protocols/preview-mode) a stage and choose a language from the **Preview language** menu above it. See [Choosing the preview language](/en/design-protocols/preview-mode#choosing-the-preview-language). A text with no translation in the preview language appears as it would to a participant using that language.

## Printing the summary in a language

The **Summary** tab shows your whole protocol on one page, which you can print with the **Print** button. When your protocol has more than one language, choose which language the summary shows your text in under **Summary language**. It starts in the default language. This is useful for sharing a translation with a colleague or an ethics board, or for checking it on paper.

## How participants get their language

When an interview starts, it uses the first language in the participant's language settings that your protocol is written in. Close regional matches count, so a participant whose browser is set to Mexican Spanish (`es-MX`) is shown your Spanish (`es`) text, and one set to European Portuguese (`pt-PT`) is shown your Brazilian Portuguese (`pt-BR`) text. If none of the participant's languages matches one of your protocol's languages, the interview uses the default language.

This is the language the interview is shown in, and participants see each text in it wherever your protocol has a translation. Where it doesn't, they see another language's translation instead (see [The default language and which translation participants see](#the-default-language-and-which-translation-participants-see)).

Where those settings come from depends on the app:

- **Fresco** uses the languages set in the participant's web browser. The language of the Fresco dashboard doesn't affect interviews.
- **Interviewer** uses the language settings of the device or browser it runs on. Interviewer's own **App language** setting doesn't affect interviews.

To let participants choose for themselves, add a [Language Chooser](/en/design-protocols/interface-documentation/language-chooser) stage to your protocol. It lists your protocol's languages alphabetically, each by its own name, and it is the only way for a participant to change the language during an interview. Once they choose, the interview keeps their choice, even when it is resumed later. Until then, the interview picks a language again each time it is opened, so an interview resumed on a different device can appear in a different language.

The interview's own buttons and messages follow the same settings, separately from your protocol's text. They are available in English, English (UK), Spanish, German, French, Italian, Dutch, Brazilian Portuguese, Simplified Chinese and Traditional Chinese. When none of the participant's languages is one of these, they are shown in English.

When the interview is shown in a right-to-left language, such as Arabic or Hebrew, its stages are laid out from right to left.

## Languages in your data

Each interview records the language its text was last shown in, and your exported data includes it. See [Interview language](/en/analyze-data/data-export#interview-language).
